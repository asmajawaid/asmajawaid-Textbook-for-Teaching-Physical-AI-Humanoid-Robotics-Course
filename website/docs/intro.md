# Welcome to the Course

This section contains the full textbook for the "Physical AI & Humanoid Robotics Course."

### **Introduction**

**Teaching Physical AI and Humanoid Robotics** is a structured, spec-driven textbook designed to bridge the gap between theoretical artificial intelligence and its real-world embodiment in robotic systems. While much AI education focuses on abstract models and software-only environments, this course emphasizes **Physical AI**—intelligent behavior that emerges when algorithms interact with sensors, actuators, physics, and real environments.

The book is built around the idea that **humanoid robotics is a systems engineering problem**, not just a machine learning problem. A humanoid robot integrates perception, decision-making, control, and embodiment within strict physical and computational constraints. Teaching such systems effectively requires a methodology that is **reproducible, testable, and simulation-first**.

To achieve this, the course adopts:

* **ROS 2 as the core middleware**, enabling distributed, modular robot software
* **Modern simulation environments** (Gazebo, Unity, and NVIDIA Isaac Sim) to create high-fidelity digital twins
* **Spec-first development workflows**, ensuring clarity, traceability, and academic rigor
* **Hands-on, executable examples**, where every concept is reinforced through runnable code and simulations

The textbook is organized into progressive modules, starting from ROS 2 foundations and advancing toward full humanoid simulation, perception, and sim-to-real considerations. Each module is designed to stand on its own while contributing to a coherent end-to-end understanding of physical AI systems.

This course is intended for:

* University students in robotics, AI, and mechatronics
* Educators designing modern robotics curricula
* Engineers transitioning from software AI to embodied intelligence

By the end of the course, readers will not only understand *how* humanoid robots are built, but *why* modern physical AI systems are architected the way they are—and how to design, simulate, and reason about them with professional rigor.
